## Abstract

We prove a conditional reduction for the Erdős–Straus conjecture. The reduction is elementary and complete: given the hypothesis (H24) that every prime `p ≡ 1 (mod 24)` admits positive integers `x, y, z` with `4/p = 1/x + 1/y + 1/z`, the conjecture holds for all `n ≥ 2`. The proof has two components of the formal reduction: (i) a scaling lemma showing that a solution for a prime factor lifts to all of its multiples — once (H24) and Theorem 4.2 supply representations for the primes, every composite inherits one — which removes the composite portion of the resistant class from consideration entirely; and (ii) a mod-chain lemma showing that the certified 7-case partition of the conjecture, combined with the known prime case `n ≡ 5 (mod 8)`, leaves exactly the primes `≡ 1 (mod 24)` uncovered. Both components are formalized in Lean 4 with no new axioms — Appendix A audits the actual axiom dependency — and zero `sorry` declarations (`ErdosStrausConjecture.Reduction`); the companion constructive criterion (`CenteredCriterion`), which converts any span witness of the u-method into an explicit solution, is likewise fully formalized. We then analyze the remaining hypothesis (H24): its span-criterion translation, the constraints a covering argument must satisfy (the Mordell non-residue obstruction; the span-reachability obstacle documented for the layers `u = 31` and `u = 35`), the effectiveness available from classical least-quadratic-non-residue bounds (Burgess, Norton) and the GRH bound (Ankeny), and the empirical record — a verified certificate layer of 166,011 primes `≡ 1 (mod 12)` up to 10,000,000 (82,887 of them `≡ 1 (mod 24)`, the class (H24) concerns) in which the largest first-working parameter is `A = 107`.

## 1. Introduction

The Erdős–Straus conjecture states that for every integer `n ≥ 2`, there exist positive integers `x, y, z` such that

$$\frac{4}{n} = \frac{1}{x} + \frac{1}{y} + \frac{1}{z}.$$

Despite work since 1950 (Erdős, Mordell, Vaughan, Elsholtz–Tao, Salez, Bright–Loughran, Mballa), the conjecture remains open. The classical obstruction to identity-based attacks is the Mordell (1969) principle: a polynomial identity providing solutions for all `n ≡ r (mod m)` can exist only when `r` is a quadratic non-residue modulo `m`. Since 1 is a quadratic residue modulo every `m`, the class `n ≡ 1 (mod 12)` — which contains the resistant cases — is closed to finite systems of uniform polynomial identities (the Mordell obstruction, as stated precisely in §6.4); it remains open whether *other* congruence-based approaches (e.g. the covering-system reformulation of Bloom–Elsholtz) can succeed there.

The present paper sharpens *where* the conjecture's remaining content lives. Its starting point is the certified toolkit of the companion manuscript v13: the six modular identities covering every `n ≢ 1 (mod 12)` (together with `n ≡ 1 (mod 12)` with `5 ∣ n`), Theorem 2 covering the prime case `n ≡ 5 (mod 8)`, and a machine-verified case partition. We add:

- **The scaling lemma** (§3): a solution for `n` lifts to a solution for `n·k` for every `k ≥ 1`; consequently every composite inherits a solution from a prime factor provided that factor has one, and only prime residue classes can be intrinsically hard.
- **The mod-chain lemma** (§4): `n ≡ 1 (mod 12)` and `n ≢ 1 (mod 24)` force `n ≡ 5 (mod 8)` — so for primes, the certified Theorem 2 case (§2.3) meets the resistant class in exactly one residue, leaving `n ≡ 1 (mod 24)` as the sole uncovered prime class.
- **The conditional reduction** (§5): hence the full conjecture follows from (H24), the statement that every prime `≡ 1 (mod 24)` has a representation.

Everything in §2–§5 is machine-verified: the partition, the identities, the constructive criterion, the scaling lemma, the mod-chain lemma, the reduction, and **both directions of Theorem 5.2 — composed into a single machine-checked biconditional** (the constructive reverse `u_method_witness` in `CenteredCriterion.lean`; the witness-extraction `witness_extraction` and the composed equivalence `finite_search_equivalence` in `FiniteSearch.lean`, the latter added at v1.4/v1.5 per external review V14-03 — the ten-module CI build set: `DivisorResidue`, `ClassicalIdentities`, `OmegaCases`, `CenteredEquivalence`, `MainTheorem`, `Theorem4`, `NewTheorems`, `CenteredCriterion`, `Reduction`, `FiniteSearch`; 0 `sorry` declarations in the new results; 2 documented axioms in the context modules). Section 6 is honest analysis, not theorem: it states what (H24) means, what the classical analytic results do and do not supply toward it, and what the verified computational record shows.

## 2. Notation and the certified toolkit

Throughout, `IsErdosStraus n` abbreviates: there exist positive integers `x, y, z` with `4·x·y·z = n·(x·y + x·z + y·z)`, i.e. `4/n = 1/x + 1/y + 1/z`.

The reduction assembles five components, all previously formalized and CI-enforced:

**2.1 The seven-case partition.** Every `n ≥ 2` falls into exactly one of seven residue cases: `n ≡ 0 (mod 3)`; `n ≡ 2 (mod 3)`; `n ≡ 4, 7, 10 (mod 12)`; `n ≡ 1 (mod 12)` with `5 ∣ n`; and `n ≡ 1 (mod 12)` with `5 ∤ n`. The first five are solved by the identities of §2.2, the sixth explicitly, and the seventh splits further. Machine-checked as `residue_classification` (`MainTheorem.lean`).

**2.2 The modular identities.** Each coverable case has an explicit two- or three-term representation; for example, for `n = 3k + 2`,

$$\frac{4}{n} = \frac{1}{n} + \frac{1}{k+1} + \frac{1}{n(k+1)},$$

verified by clearing denominators. All six cases (`mod_3_zero`, `mod_3_two`, `mod_12_four`, `mod_12_seven`, `mod_12_ten`, `mod_12_one_mod_5_zero`) are proven in Lean in `Identities.lean` — the module the case-partition theorems actually import.

**2.3 Theorem 2, with its explicit identity.** Every prime `n ≡ 5 (mod 8)` — indeed
every integer `n ≡ 5 (mod 8)`, primality unnecessary — has `A = 3`:
$$\frac{4}{n} = \frac{1}{\frac{n+3}{4}} + \frac{1}{\frac{n(n+3)}{8}} + \frac{1}{\frac{n(n+3)}{4}}.$$
Clearing denominators — with `A = (n+3)/4`, `B = n(n+3)/8`, `C = n(n+3)/4`: `4·A·B·C − n·(B·C + A·C + A·B) = n²(n+3)³/32 − n·n(n+3)³/32 = 0` — verified directly by `linear_combination` in Lean (`CenteredEquivalence.lean`, `theorem2`); each denominator is a positive integer since `n ≡ 5 (mod 8)` makes `n + 3` divisible by 8.

**2.4 The residual-class lemma.** The 7-case partition refines to: everything is covered except

$$C = \{n ≥ 13 :\; n ≡ 1 \pmod{12},\; 5 \nmid n,\; \neg(n \text{ prime} \wedge n ≡ 5 \pmod 8)\}.$$

This is `erdos_straus_except_residual_open_class` (`MainTheorem.lean`), the strongest existing honest coverage theorem of the program. Inside `C`, the elementary facts resolve the set further into the prime class `n ≡ 1 (mod 24)` and the composites `≡ 1 (mod 12)` with `5 ∤ n`.

**2.5 The constructive criterion.** For odd `u ≡ 3 (mod 4)` with `u = 4x − n > 0` and `x = (n+u)/4` a positive integer: whenever there is a positive divisor `P ∣ (n·x)²` with `P ≡ −n·x (mod u)` and `gcd(u, n·x) = 1`, the construction

$$x = \frac{n+u}{4}, \quad y = \frac{P + n x}{u}, \quad z = \frac{Q + n x}{u}, \quad Q = \frac{(n x)^2}{P}$$

is positive-integer-valued and satisfies `4·x·y·z = n·(x·y + x·z + y·z)` identically. This is `u_method_core` and its constructive wrapper in `CenteredCriterion.lean` (0 `sorry`). In span language: with `M = n·x` and `gcd(M, u) = 1` as required above, the divisor condition on `P` is equivalent to `P·M^{-1} ≡ −1 (mod u)`; a span witness with exponent coefficients in `[−e_j, e_j]` relative to the prime factorization of `M` becomes the divisor `P ∣ M²` (exponents in `[0, 2e_j]`) after multiplication by `M`, and conversely. §6.1 states this precisely.

## 3. The scaling lemma

**Lemma 3.1 (scaling).** If `4/n = 1/x + 1/y + 1/z` with `x, y, z > 0` and `k ≥ 1`, then

$$\frac{4}{nk} = \frac{1}{kx} + \frac{1}{ky} + \frac{1}{kz}.$$

*Proof.* The starting identity is `4·x·y·z = n·(x·y + x·z + y·z)`. Multiplying through by `k³`,

$$4(kx)(ky)(kz) = k^3 \cdot 4xyz = k^3 \cdot n(xy + xz + yz) = (nk)\big((kx)(ky) + (kx)(kz) + (ky)(kz)\big),$$

which is the defining identity for `(nk, kx, ky, kz)`, with every denominator positive. ∎

**Lemma 3.2 (prime descent).** Every composite `n ≥ 2` has a prime divisor `p` with `n = p·(n/p)` and `n/p ≥ 1`.

*Proof.* Standard (every `m ≠ 1` has a prime divisor); formalized via `Nat.exists_prime_and_dvd`. ∎

**Corollary 3.3 (composites inherit; the hard core is prime).** If every prime has a representation, then every composite `n ≥ 2` has one: take a prime factor `p ∣ n` (Lemma 3.2), a representation for `p` (hypothesis), and scale by `n/p` via Lemma 3.1.

In Lean this is the composite branch of `full_conjecture_of_h24` (`Reduction.lean`), with `Reduction.scaling` proved by `ring`-normalization after rewriting through the hypothesis.

The consequence is worth stating plainly: **composite residue classes cannot be intrinsically hard.** Whatever resists the conjecture resists only on primes.

## 4. The mod-chain: why exactly 1 (mod 24)

**Lemma 4.1 (mod-chain).** If `n ≡ 1 (mod 12)` and `n ≢ 1 (mod 24)`, then `n ≡ 5 (mod 8)`.

*Proof.* Since `12 ∣ 24`, `n ≡ 1 (mod 12)` gives `n mod 24 ∈ {1, 13}` with `0 ≤ n mod 24 < 24`; by hypothesis it is not `1`, so `n ≡ 13 (mod 24)`. Writing `n = 24·⌊n/24⌋ + 13` and rearranging,

$$n = 5 + \left(1 + 3\left\lfloor \tfrac{n}{24}\right\rfloor\right)\cdot 8,$$

gives `n ≡ 5 (mod 8)` directly. ∎

(This lemma is `mod8_of_12` in `Reduction.lean`. The proof carries the explicit rearrangement above rather than trusting `omega` across the three moduli — the tactic does not chain them, and it may fail on goals involving unfolded primality definitions; see Appendix A for the recorded pitfall.)

**Theorem 4.2 (unconditional prime coverage).** Every prime `n ≥ 2` with `n ≢ 1 (mod 24)` has a representation.

*Proof.* Let `n` be prime with `n ≢ 1 (mod 24)`.

- If `n ≢ 1 (mod 12)`: since `n` is prime, `n = 2`, `n = 3`, or `n ≡ 5, 7, 11 (mod 12)`. The residues `5` and `11 (mod 12)` fall under case 2 of the partition (`n ≡ 2 (mod 3)`), as does `n = 2`; the residue `7 (mod 12)` falls under case 3 (`n ≡ 4, 7, 10 (mod 12)`); and `n = 3` falls under case 1 (`n ≡ 0 (mod 3)`). All are covered by the identities of §2.2 — so `n` is covered.
- If `n ≡ 1 (mod 12)`: then by Lemma 4.1 (and `n ≢ 1 (mod 24)`) we get `n ≡ 5 (mod 8)`, and Theorem 2 (§2.3) applies — `n` is prime and `≡ 5 (mod 8)`.

∎ In Lean, this is `prime_non24_isErdosStraus`: the `h_not_open` guard of the residual-class lemma becomes automatic, because the only configuration in which that guard fails for a prime — `n ≡ 1 (mod 12)`, `5 ∤ n`, `n ≥ 13`, not `(prime ∧ n ≡ 5 (mod 8))` — is exactly the `≡ 1 (mod 24)` class, which is excluded here by assumption.

Theorem 4.2 is **unconditional**: no hypothesis is used, and the proof is elementary throughout.

## 5. The conditional reduction

**Theorem 5.1 (reduction).** Suppose (H24): *every prime `p ≡ 1 (mod 24)` admits positive integers `x, y, z` with `4/p = 1/x + 1/y + 1/z`.* Then every `n ≥ 2` admits a representation.

*Proof.* Let `n ≥ 2`.

- If `n` is prime: if `n ≡ 1 (mod 24)`, (H24) applies; otherwise Theorem 4.2 applies.
- If `n` is composite: by Corollary 3.3, `n` inherits from any prime factor `p ∣ n`; that prime again falls to (H24) or Theorem 4.2 by the prime branch.

∎ Machine-checked as `full_conjecture_of_h24` (`Reduction.lean`). There is no circularity: the composite branch consults only prime factors, and every prime terminates in either (H24) or a certified identity.

**Remarks.**

1. The reduction also shows the *documented axiom's* class statement (`n ≡ 1 (mod 12), 5 ∤ n, n ≥ 13` assumed wholesale in the Lean development) is formally stronger than necessary: only its prime `≡ 1 (mod 24)` part is genuinely open, and the remaining content is machine-checked to follow from (H24).
2. Via §2.5, (H24) for a given prime `p` is *effective in form*: it suffices to exhibit, at any admissible `u`, a divisor `P` satisfying the span condition — the machine-checked wrapper then converts the span witness into an explicit solution. *(Restated per the third external review, V12-02.)* Theorem 5.2 itself already establishes a **finite decision procedure for each residual prime**: enumerate the finite parameter domain `3 ≤ u ≤ 2p, u ≡ 3 (mod 4)`; for each, enumerate the divisors of `M²` and test the span condition; return a solution if one exists, a certified negative result otherwise (given a correct implementation). Termination is therefore unconditional — it does not depend on (H24). What a proof of (H24) would guarantee is that the procedure **always returns a positive result**; and only a stronger quantitative covering theorem (`F(p) = O(log p)`, `O((log p)²)`, or a universal constant — open research questions, §6.3) would return substantially smaller search bounds and improve efficiency.

**Theorem 5.2 (finite-search equivalence).** Fix an odd prime `p ≡ 1 (mod 4)` (the (H24) class `p ≡ 1 (mod 24)` is the motivating instance; per the third external review, V12-06, the theorem does not use the `mod 24` restriction). Then `p` has an Erdős–Straus representation **if and only if** there exist `u` and a divisor `P` with

$$0 < u \le 2p, \qquad u \equiv 3 \pmod 4, \qquad \gcd(u, M) = 1 \ (M = px,\ x = (p+u)/4), \qquad P \mid M^2, \qquad P \equiv -M \pmod u.$$

*Domain remark (per review §4.4).* The admissible `u` satisfying `0 < u ≤ 2p, u ≡ 3 (mod 4)` are exactly `{3, 7, 11, …, 2p−3}` — `(p−1)/2` of them — and no member is divisible by `p` (the only multiples of `p` in range are `p` itself, `≡ 1 (mod 4)`, and `2p ≡ 2 (mod 4)`).

*Proof.* (⟸) is the constructive criterion of §2.5. (⟹) suppose `4/p = 1/x + 1/y + 1/z`, all positive. The defining identity `4·x·y·z = p(xy + xz + yz)` is fully symmetric in `x, y, z`, so reorder with `x` the smallest denominator. Then `4/p = 1/x + 1/y + 1/z > 1/x` gives `x > p/4`, and `4/p ≤ 3/x` gives `x ≤ 3p/4`; setting `u = 4x − p` yields `0 < u ≤ 2p`, `u ≡ −p ≡ 3 (mod 4)`. **Coprimality (the step added per review §4.2):** any common divisor `d` of `u` and `p` divides `4x = u + p`; since `p` is an odd prime, `d` is `1` or `p`, and `d = p` would force `p ∣ 4x`, hence `p ∣ x` (as `p ∤ 4`), contradicting `0 < x < p` — so `gcd(u, p) = 1`. The same argument with a common divisor of `u` and `x` (it divides `4x − u = p`, and would be `p` only if `p ∣ x`, again impossible) gives `gcd(u, x) = 1`; hence `gcd(u, M) = gcd(u, px) = 1` as the theorem requires. The witness is explicit: `P := u·y − M` and `Q := u·z − M` satisfy `P·z = p·x·y` and `Q·y = p·x·z` (one-line consequences of the defining identity), hence are positive, and `P·Q·y·z = (P·z)(Q·y) = p²x²y·z`, i.e. `P·Q = M²`. Finally `P = u·y − M ≡ −M (mod u)` additively. ∎

*Scope note (V12-06, continued).* The smallest-denominator argument and the divisor-factorization method are classical; the paper claims no originality for them. The contribution is the machine-checked **composition**: the extraction (`witness_extraction`, module `FiniteSearch.lean` — committed in v1.3, 0 sorries, standard axioms only) with the constructive reverse (`u_method_witness`, `CenteredCriterion.lean`) together make both directions of this equivalence Lean-verified, and fold the per-prime search into the span framework of §6.1. The open problem is then exactly: *for every prime `p ≡ 1 (mod 24)` the finite domain `{3, 7, …, 2p−3}` contains a successful parameter.*

## 6. (H24): what is and is not known

This section is analysis, not theorem. It is included because (H24) is now, by Theorem 5.1, the precise remaining content of the conjecture.

**6.1 The span-criterion form.** For a prime `p ≡ 1 (mod 24)` and an admissible parameter `u ≡ 3 (mod 4)` with `p ∤ u`, put `M = p·(p+u)/4` (= `p·x`, so `gcd(M, u) = 1`: a common divisor of `x = (p+u)/4` and `u` divides `4x = p + u` and hence also `p`, and `p ∤ u`). By the one-divisor reduction (`CENTERED_PRODUCT_SET_THEOREM.md`; §2.5), the constructive criterion is equivalent to

$$\exists\, P \mid M^2 \;\;\text{with}\;\; P \equiv -M \;(\mathrm{mod}\ u) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \exists\, P \mid M^2 \;\;\text{with}\;\; P\cdot M^{-1} \equiv -1 \;(\mathrm{mod}\ u).$$

Writing the prime factorization `M = ∏_{j=1}^{r} q_j^{e_j}`, the right-hand condition is the *span condition*: define the **centered product set** in the unit group `(ℤ/uℤ)×`,

$$\mathcal{S}_u(M) \;=\; \prod_{j=1}^{r}\,\bigl\{\,q_j^{-e_j},\; q_j^{-(e_j-1)},\; \ldots,\; q_j^{e_j-1},\; q_j^{e_j}\,\bigr\},$$

i.e. the set of all products `∏_j q_j^{t_j} (mod u)` with integer coefficients `t_j ∈ [−e_j, e_j]`. Then `u` succeeds for `(p, u)` iff `−1 ∈ 𝒮_u(M)`. The two divisor formulations match exactly: a divisor `P ∣ M²` has exponents `b_j ∈ [0, 2e_j]`, and putting `t_j = b_j − e_j` gives `P·M^{-1} = ∏_j q_j^{t_j}` — so multiplying the congruence `P ≡ −M (mod u)` by the unit `M^{-1}` (invertible because `gcd(M, u) = 1`) turns it into `P·M^{-1} ≡ −1 (mod u)`; conversely, any span witness `∏_j q_j^{t_j} ≡ −1 (mod u)` yields the divisor `P = ∏_j q_j^{t_j + e_j} ∣ M²` with `P ≡ −M (mod u)`. The permitted coefficient range is therefore exactly `[−e_j, e_j]`, matching the exponent window `[0, 2e_j]` of the divisors of `M²`.

**Cyclicity caveat (this subsection replaces the earlier single-generator formulation).** `(ℤ/uℤ)×` is a finite abelian group and is *not* cyclic in general; a discrete-logarithm phrasing to a single generator `g` is available only when the group happens to be cyclic — e.g. `(ℤ/35ℤ)× ≅ (ℤ/5ℤ)× × (ℤ/7ℤ)× ≅ C₄ × C₆` is noncyclic (it has no element of order 24). When `u` is an odd prime power — in particular every prime `u` (the prime-parameter layers `3, 7, 11, 19, 23, 31, 47, 59, 71, 79, 83, 103, 107, 127` of the repository's layer documents, and the composite prime power `u = 27 = 3³`) — `(ℤ/uℤ)×` is cyclic of order `φ(u)`: for a generator `g`, with `k_j = dlog_g(q_j mod u)` and `−1 = g^{φ(u)/2}`, the condition reads `∑_j k_j·t_j ≡ φ(u)/2 (mod φ(u))` for some `t_j ∈ [−e_j, e_j]` — a bounded modular integer combination (per the fourth review's V13-07 terminology note). In general, decompose `u = ∏_ℓ u_ℓ` into pairwise-coprime prime powers; by CRT, `(ℤ/uℤ)× ≅ ∏_ℓ (ℤ/u_ℓℤ)×`, and the condition is *coupled*: the same coefficients `t_j` must realize `−1` in every component simultaneously (since `−1 (mod u)` maps to `(−1, …, −1)` under the CRT isomorphism). `u = 35` is the worked example: the coupled condition in `(ℤ/5ℤ)× × (ℤ/7ℤ)×` is the correct formulation, and no single-generator version exists. The certificate dataset itself exercises the noncyclic case: 991 of its 166,011 records have a noncyclic first-working parameter `u ∈ {15, 35, 39, 51, 55}` (of 1,082 records whose first-working parameter is composite, the remainder is the cyclic prime power `u = 27`). The layer analyses `MOD31_EXACT_REDUCTION.md` and `MOD35_CRT_SPAN.md` document this condition exactly, with zero mismatch across the certified stages (at `u = 31`: 283 stage entrants, 0 span mismatches; at `u = 35`: the coupled condition in `(ℤ/5)× × (ℤ/7)×`, 93 entrants, 0 mismatches; a 2026-11-19 reconciliation, `FIRST_A_STRUCTURE.md`, recomputed the stage chains with the full admissible layer set — the documented chain omitted `u = 19` and `u = 27`, and the true residual-class stages are 217 solvable-152 at `u = 31` and 65 solvable-17 at `u = 35`, terminating identically).

**6.2 What least-non-residue bounds give.** The classical effective inputs are:

- *Burgess (1962) via Norton (1971):* the least prime quadratic non-residue `q` modulo `p` satisfies `q ≪ p^{1/(4√e) + ε}` for every `ε > 0`.
- *Ankeny (1952), under GRH:* the least non-residue is `O((log p)²)`.

These guarantee that a *non-residue* arrives fast. Obtaining a non-residue in a *prescribed residue class* is a strictly stronger demand and would need its own effective input — e.g. a GRH-conditional Ankeny-type bound for the least prime in a prescribed coset of `(ℤ/uℤ)×`, or uniform Burgess-type character-sum estimates over that coset; no unconditional theorem of that strength is claimed or used here. These results also do **not** address the actual requirement of (H24): the non-residue's discrete logarithm must land in a specific coset determined by the forced factors' span — a *reachability* condition, not an existence condition (cyclic-scope: for a prime parameter `u` such as 31, `(ℤ/uℤ)×` is cyclic and the single-generator phrasing is exact; the noncyclic layers carry the coupled CRT formulation of §6.1). The `u = 31` analysis makes the distinction concrete on the corrected cohort: of the 65 true failures at that layer (first-u ≥ 35), 47 are QR-pure — blocked by the parity obstruction before any reachability question can arise — and 18 further failures are non-QR-pure yet still unreachable (the span-delicate cases, fingerprinted in `analysis/u31_layer_corrected.json`); the cube-class rescue structure, while characterizable, is not a consequence of Burgess-type bounds.

**6.3 What remains open after Theorem 5.2.** Theorem 5.2 changed the shape of the remaining objective. The search domain is no longer something to be bounded — it is **proved** finite: the complete admissible domain is `{3, 7, …, 2p−3}` (`B(p) = 2p−3` is now a theorem, not an objective, and no member needs a coprimality exclusion). Distinguishing three separate objectives (per the third external review, V12-03):

| Objective | Status |
| --- | --- |
| Establish a finite, complete search domain per prime | **Proved** (Theorem 5.2) |
| Establish that the domain always contains a witness | Open; equivalent to (H24) |
| Establish a substantially smaller sufficient bound | Open; stronger quantitative objective |

The quantitative question is now stated in a precise quantity. Define, for `p ≡ 1 (mod 24)`,
`F(p) = min{ u : −1 ∈ S_u(p(p+u)/4) }` over the admissible domain (and `∞` if none exists — but see the next objective). Then: the finite-search objective is exactly `F(p) ≤ 2p−3` **for every** such `p` (equivalent to (H24)); the quantitative objectives are `F(p) = O(log p)`, `F(p) = O((log p)²)`, or a universal constant bound — these are **research questions, not established results**, and the verified computational record through `10^9` alone justifies none of them (though the empirical maxima — first-working `u = 83` against `2p = 575,134,562` at `p = 287,567,281`, and `107` against `2p = 17,606,738` at `p = 8,803,369` — suggest the bound gap is enormous and worth attacking). The instructive case in the data, `p = 8,803,369`, was settled by computation (first working `A = 107`) — a parameter that may have antecedents in the literature this repository has not verified: the Ionascu–Wilson framework (Rev. Roumaine Math. Pures Appl. 56 (2011), 21–30) already bounds the first denominator and tabulates exceptional prime classes, so this program claims no novelty for the record itself.

**6.4 The Mordell constraint on identity-hunting.** By the Mordell (1969) principle — the framework being *polynomial identities*: parametric solutions `4/n = 1/x(n) + 1/y(n) + 1/z(n)` with `x(n), y(n), z(n)` polynomials in `n` (rational, in particular integer, coefficients), required to give solutions for **every** integer `n` in an entire residue class `n ≡ r (mod m)` — no such identity exists for a class whose representative is a quadratic residue modulo `m` (i.e. whenever the congruence `x² ≡ r (mod m)` is solvable). The obstruction is uniform: no polynomial identity, and hence no finite system of identities, can cover the `≡ 1`-classes, because `1` is a quadratic residue modulo every modulus. Two scope qualifications are in order: the obstruction is about **finite systems of uniform polynomial identities** — it does not eliminate more general *covering systems* (the survey of Bloom and Elsholtz (2022) reformulates the conjecture as a coverage claim over congruence classes, a potentially infinite covering system that the obstruction does not rule out), nor does it eliminate the span-route or other structural mechanisms. The route to (H24) therefore cannot finish purely by extending the identity table; this repository's route goes through the span/reachability structure of §6.1 — the route its data are built for — while covering-system routes along the Bloom–Elsholtz reformulation remain open alternatives.

**One-sided characterization of the research population (per sixth review §4; formalized at v1.6).** With `M = p·x` and the witness-pair construction `y = (P + M)/u`, `z = (M²/P + M)/u`, the ratio is exactly `z/y = M/P` (multiplying by `u`: `z·u = M²/P + M = (M/P)·(P + M)`), hence

> **`y ∣ z  ⟺  P ∣ M`.**

A parametric witness can therefore be detected from the divisors of `M` alone, without constructing both denominators. Writing `P = M/d`, the required congruences reduce to `d ≡ −1 (mod u)` (cancel the unit `P` modulo `u`; `gcd(M, u) = 1` by admissibility), which gives the review's **one-sided product-set criterion**: a parametric witness exists for `(p, u)` iff **`−1 ∈ 𝒟_u(M)`**, where `𝒟_u(M) = { Π qⱼ^{tⱼ} mod u : 0 ≤ tⱼ ≤ eⱼ }` is the set of divisor residues of `M` — in contrast with the paper's centered product set `𝒮_u(M)`, which spans products of divisor residues and their negations symmetrically. The review's proposed research population (sixth review §4.2) is therefore characterized exactly by the set-difference

> **`−1 ∈ 𝒮_u(M)` and `−1 ∉ 𝒟_u(M)`** at the first-working `u` — the 89,083 primes are precisely those where centered reachability succeeds but one-sided reachability does not.

Both statements are now machine-verified in `FiniteSearch.lean` ( Lean axioms `[propext, Quot.sound]` and `[propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound]` respectively, in the 13-result CI audit): `parametric_divisibility` (under the paper's witness conditions, `T ∣ M` implies `y ∣ z` with quotient `M/T`) and `param_exists_iff_onesided` (parametric-witness existence ⟺ some divisor `d ∣ M` satisfies `(d + 1) mod u = 0`, i.e. `−1 ∈ 𝒟_u(M)`). ## 7. Conclusion

The Erdős–Straus conjecture is logically equivalent to the single statement (H24) about primes `≡ 1 (mod 24)`. One direction is trivial; the other is Theorem 5.1, whose proof is elementary, complete, and machine-checked with no new axioms. The reduction's practical value: it eliminates the composite portion of the resistant class with no casework (scaling, §3); it shows the certified partition meets the resistant class in exactly one prime residue (§4); Theorem 5.2 bounds the per-prime search domain (`0 < u ≤ 2p`, finite); and it converts any future analytic progress toward (H24) — a covering theorem over the span classes of §6.1, or a GRH-conditional span-reachability result — into solutions, because the criterion-to-solution direction is constructive and formalized (§2.5).

The conjecture is not proved, here or in the literature; (H24) is open, and the honest open problem it poses is now shaped by Theorem 5.2: the per-prime search domain is **proved** finite (`{3, 7, …, 2p−3}`), so what remains is to prove the *witness-existence* half — that the domain always contains a span-reachable witness, i.e. `F(p) ≤ 2p−3` for every prime `p ≡ 1 (mod 24)` (§6.3) — or the stronger quantitative form of the same statement. This is a sharper target than the conjecture itself, because every surrounding combinatorial ingredient is now either certified or reduced away.
